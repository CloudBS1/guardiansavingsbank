How to Buy a House Before Selling Yours: Blanket Loan, Bridge Loan, HELOC or Contingency

You've found the house. The trouble is, you still live in the one you need to sell to pay for it. If you write an offer that depends on your sale, the seller may pick someone else. If you sell first, you might be renting a storage unit and an apartment while you shop.

There are four common ways around this, and they differ in cost, risk and convenience. Below we compare them side by side, explain how a lender looks at two house payments, and show where a blanket loan, an option Guardian Savings Bank offers, fits in.

The short answer: buying before you sell comes down to two things: covering the down payment without your sale proceeds, and qualifying while you own both homes, or using a loan built to handle that overlap.

Two problems you need to solve

Buying before selling creates two separate questions.

  1. Where does the down payment come from? For many move-up buyers, the down payment is tied up as equity in their current home.
  2. Can you qualify with both homes on the books? Until your current home closes, a lender usually has to count its payment along with the new one. Fannie Mae's guidelines say both payments must be used to qualify you unless you provide an executed sales contract on the current home (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-6-06).

Every option below solves one or both of those problems in a different way.

Option 1: A blanket loan

A blanket loan is a single mortgage secured by more than one property. For a move-up buyer, it lets you use the equity in your current home toward the down payment on the new one, so you don't need a contingent offer and you aren't juggling separate loans.

At Guardian, a basic blanket loan gives you one loan, one closing and one payment covering both homes, and lets you move into the new house before the old one sells. Interest-only choices are available.

How it works in practice: both your current home and the new one secure the loan, so the equity you've built in the home you're leaving can stand in for the down payment you would otherwise wait on. How much you can borrow against the combined value depends on underwriting, so ask a loan officer to walk through your own numbers.

Good fit if: you have meaningful equity, want a non-contingent offer, and would rather have one payment and one lender than a stack of short-term loans.

Watch out for: until your home sells you are carrying debt on both properties, and both homes are pledged as collateral. Price your current home realistically and have a plan if it sits longer than expected. Ask your loan officer exactly what happens to the loan and your payment once the old home sells.

Option 2: A bridge loan

A bridge loan is a short-term loan, usually secured by your current home, that supplies the down payment for the new one. You repay it when the old house sells.

Bridge loans work, but they add a third loan. If your new mortgage is a conventional loan sold to Fannie Mae, the bridge loan can't be cross-collateralized against the new property, and the lender must document that you can carry the payments on the new home, the current home and the bridge loan together (Fannie Mae Selling Guide B3-4.3-14). Bridge loans generally cost more than a standard mortgage and come due on a short schedule. If your home hasn't sold when the bridge comes due, that balance has to be repaid or extended.

Good fit if: your home is expected to sell soon and you have the income to cover three payments in the meantime.

Option 3: A home equity line of credit (HELOC) on your current home

A HELOC lets you borrow against the equity in your home, up to a credit limit, during a draw period (CFPB). You draw the down payment, buy the new house, then pay the line off when the old one sells.

The catch is timing. HELOCs are generally made on owner-occupied homes, so you need to open the line before you list your house. And the HELOC payment counts against your debt-to-income ratio on the new mortgage, alongside your existing first mortgage. HELOCs also usually carry a variable rate, so the payment can change month to month.

At Guardian, HELOCs are available to homeowners whose first mortgage is with Guardian Savings Bank.

Good fit if: you plan ahead, have plenty of equity and enough income to qualify with three payments.

Option 4: A contingent offer (or a rent-back)

A home-sale contingency makes your purchase dependent on selling your current home by a certain date. It is the most conservative route financially: if your house doesn't sell, you can walk away under the contract terms. The cost is competitiveness, because sellers often prefer offers that don't hinge on another sale.

A close cousin is selling first with a rent-back (also called a post-closing occupancy agreement), where your buyer lets you stay for a set period after closing. You get your equity in cash, then shop with a firm offer, but you'll be on a deadline.

How lenders look at your income when you own two homes

This is where many buy-before-sell plans fall apart, so it's worth running numbers early.

  • Both payments count until the old home closes, unless the lender's rules let them exclude it (for example, a signed sales contract with financing contingencies cleared, under Fannie Mae's guidelines).
  • Reserves matter. Lenders want to see that you can weather a slow sale, so cash left over after closing helps.

That's why we suggest talking with a loan officer before you start touring homes. Our mortgage checklist lists the documents to gather.

A practical game plan

  1. Price your current home honestly. Get a realistic sale price and time on market for your neighborhood, not the most optimistic one.
  2. Talk to a loan officer early. The right route depends on your equity, income and how long you can carry two homes.
  3. Decide what happens if the old house sits. Know your monthly cost with both homes and how many months you can cover it.
  4. Line up the timing. If you choose a HELOC, open it before listing. If you choose a blanket loan, ask how the loan will be handled once the sale closes.
  5. Write the offer. With financing settled, you can make a stronger, non-contingent offer.
